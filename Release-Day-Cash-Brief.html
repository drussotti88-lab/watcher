Design brief · intent only

The alert should know what you already owe

Two halves — finding product before it's gone, and knowing what your buying has already committed you to. Everything durable about both, separated from the implementation choices of the first build. No language, no schema, no service.

The job

Two sentences, and every decision should trace to one of them.

Half A · acquisition

Tell me a product I want is buyable, early enough and specifically enough that I can actually act on it.

Half B · obligation

Tell me how much money is going to leave my account on a given day, and which purchases make it up — without me having to remember anything.

Neither sentence mentions email, Discord, Pokémon, or a dashboard. Those are all means. What Half B does say, implicitly, is the hard part: without me having to remember anything. A tool you have to feed is a tool you stop feeding by week three, and receipts landing in an inbox are the only thing that reliably already happens.

One row needs care, because I initially read too much into it. Guppy's mission screen shows "7 drops rumored — you're armed for 6 · est. worst case if all hit: $504.78". That's a dollar figure with a sum behind it, and I first called it a money view. It isn't one.

Look at what it's scoped to. One event, one night. Only missions you armed. A hypothetical maximum — if all six hit. And it evaporates once the drop is over. It answers a single narrow question, well: am I comfortable with tonight's worst case before I go to bed? That's a pre-flight check on one sortie, not an account of what you owe.

Half B is a different object entirely: every purchase, every retailer, however you bought it, organised by the date money actually moves, accumulating rather than expiring. Guppy's number can't answer "what clears on the 26th" — it has no concept of a date money lands on, because it never looks past tonight.

The part that can't be closed by a competitor

Guppy can only ever count what Guppy armed, on the night it armed it. A pre-order placed by hand, a purchase on a retailer it doesn't cover, anything bought before you installed it, anything from last month — structurally invisible, because its input is its own mission list and its horizon is one evening.

Half B's input is receipts. That sees everything you buy, everywhere, forever, including the months before any of this existed. That isn't a feature gap someone ships next quarter; it's a different data source, and it's the actual moat.

A line you drew, now under review

Guppy does auto-checkout and queue-joining. Your first monitoring PRD ruled both out — detect-and-notify, human-in-the-loop. That was your call, not a law of nature, and it's reopened. See Rules & whose they are for the full ladder and where each rung actually sits.

What it costs to run

Worth knowing before choosing an architecture, because the cheap tier and the good tier turn out to be the same tier.

The expensive way

Watching a hostile retailer from a server means renting residential IPs, because datacenter addresses get blocked. Those run about $1/GB at the floor and $3–8/GB mid-market. A modern product page with its JSON payload is roughly 300KB:

1 item @ 45s polling  =  1,920 fetches/day
× ~300 KB             =  ~576 MB/day  =  ~17 GB/month
                      =  $17/mo at $1/GB · ~$50/mo at $3/GB

20 items              =  $340 – $1,000 / month

And 45 seconds isn't even competitive for a real drop. Scale it linearly for anything faster.

The free way, which is also better

A browser on your own machine, on your own residential connection, with real cookies and a real session, is the exact thing anti-bot systems are trying to verify. It isn't an imitation of a legitimate visitor — it is one. It costs nothing, and it doesn't degrade when someone updates their fingerprinting.

This appears to be Guppy's answer too: they ship desktop apps for macOS and Windows that run locally, plus a browser extension, plus community-sourced Discord signals. The expensive, blockable work sits on users' machines. Inference from their published product, not knowledge of their infrastructure — but the shape is legible from what they chose to build.

The number that matters

Under $5/month gets you everything except centralized watching of hostile sites — and that one line item is both the most expensive thing on the list and the one with the worst return. The half nobody has built is also the half that's nearly free to run.

Rules, and whose they are

You asked to see what I've been holding. Fair. They fall into three piles, and they are not the same kind of thing — conflating them is how a preference calcifies into a law.

Pile 1 · yours

"Detect-and-notify, human-in-the-loop. No auto-checkout, no queue circumvention." This came from your own PRD §1 and §24. I read it in your README and reflected it back to you as a constraint. It was never mine, and it's entirely yours to move.

The auto-checkout ladder

"Auto-checkout" isn't one thing. Four rungs, and they're genuinely different:

#RungWhere it sits
1Deep link that opens the product, or the cart, pre-loadedNavigation. Your adapters already declare this capability.
2Autofill your own checkout form; you press BuyWhat every password manager does.
3Complete the purchase unattended — your account, your card, normal pace, queue respectedYour call. Usually a terms-of-service matter, not a legal one. Real risk is a banned account and cancelled orders.
4Defeat the bot detection — solve CAPTCHAs, spoof fingerprints, rotate IPs to look like many people, bypass the waiting roomI won't help build this.
Pile 2 · mine, and there's only one

I won't help build rung 4 — anything whose purpose is to defeat a detection or security control. That's a line about what the code is for, not about how aggressive you are. Rungs 1 through 3 are automating your own account with your own money, and I'll help with all of them.

Separately, and worth not confusing with the above: rung 3 has poor returns for you specifically. It only pays in the sub-second contest — and per A1 that contest is unwinnable for a solo builder, by Guppy's own admission about itself. The inventory you can actually get (A2) sits available for minutes, where a fast notification and your own hands are enough. So rung 3 buys you ban risk and maintenance in exchange for the segment with the worst odds. That's an engineering argument, not a moral one, and you should weigh it as such.

Pile 3 · advice, override freely

Everything else I've said is judgment, not a rule. Build the money model first. Don't hand-write an extractor. Rules as override, not on-ramp. Two halves sharing product identity. Never guess, corrections stick, compute on read, alert on transitions.

I'd defend each of them and I think they'll save you time. None is a constraint. If you want the parser hand-written or the halves fully merged, say so and I'll build it that way and stop relitigating it.

How the two halves should join

You asked me to recommend. Here it is, and the reasoning matters more than the answer.

Recommendation

Two independently-running halves that share exactly one thing: product identity. Not one merged system, not two unrelated tools.

Why not merge them. They have nothing operationally in common. One polls in seconds and is worthless if it's late; the other reads receipts and doesn't care about minutes. One fights Cloudflare; the other doesn't. Wire them together and a monitoring outage takes down your money view, which is the half that has to be trustworthy.

Why not separate them. Because the interesting questions live at the seam, and all of them are of the form "given what I already owe, should I buy this?" You can't ask that with two disconnected tools.

So share the spine, nothing else. If both halves agree on what a product is — one identity, whatever form it takes — then a restock alert can carry your existing exposure, and an order can be priced against market without either half knowing how the other works.

What's true about getting it

Properties of the problem, not of any solution.

A1

You cannot win a hyped drop by being polite, and no tool fixes that.

Guppy says this about itself. Treat the hyped drop as a lottery ticket and stop optimising for it — the effort has a terrible return, and it's the part that tempts you across the automation line.

A2

The winnable inventory is the quiet restock, not the event.

Product that sits available for ten minutes on a Tuesday is uncontested and gettable. Design for reliably catching those rather than heroically losing the big ones.

A3

Knowing before it's buyable beats knowing faster.

A SKU appearing in a catalog days before it can be bought is uncontested information — a completely different game from racing everyone to an add-to-cart. This is the most defensible edge available and it doesn't require winning any race.

And the source needn't be technical. Guppy's "7 drops rumored tonight" is attributed to a named person who posted eleven hours earlier. The best pre-drop signal in this category is often a human with good sources, not a scraper.

A4

Some retailers can't be watched from a server at all.

Anti-bot blocks datacenter IPs, so certain sites are only observable from something living in a real browser on a real machine. Any design that assumes one uniform way of watching hits this wall — and the workaround is free, which is the opposite of how these things usually go. Your extension isn't a fallback for the worker; it's the half that watches the sites that matter.

A5

Behind a queue, stock status is meaningless.

The queue is the actual gate. Knowing an item is "in stock" tells you nothing you can act on; knowing the waiting room just opened tells you everything.

A6

Local store inventory is a separate, less contested signal.

Shelf stock at a specific store is competed for by whoever is near that store, which is a far smaller field than the internet. Guppy invests here for a reason.

A7

A watch is worth more when it carries a price ceiling and a quantity.

Guppy calls it arming: not "tell me when this restocks" but "buy up to 2 of this at or under $30." Worth stealing even if you never automate the purchase, because it turns a vague watchlist into something you can add up — which is exactly what makes their worst-case number computable, and what would make yours computable too.

What's true about owing it

B1

Money leaves at a different time than you commit it.

Pre-orders authorize at purchase and charge at ship. A month of buying can produce almost no card activity, then several hundred dollars on one release day. This is why "total spent" is the wrong number and always was.

B2

Release dates are never in the order confirmation.

Not from any retailer, ever. That data comes from you, or from Half A — which is one more reason the halves should share a spine.

B3

Retailers change their email templates without warning.

Anything reading receipts is aiming at a moving target. A permanent condition, not a bug to fix once.

B4

One order can span two release months.

So the unit of money is the line item, not the order. An order-level model looks fine until the first mixed cart, then it's quietly wrong forever.

B5

Nobody spells the product the same way twice.

"Pokémon TCG: Mega Evolution Elite Trainer Box" and "Pokemon Trading Card Game Mega Evolution ETB" are the same object. This is the same problem the shared spine has to solve, from the other direction.

B6

Stores sell more than cards, so relevance is about contents, not sender. And the order number is the only stable identity — within one retailer.

What building Half B once actually taught me

These cost something to learn. They're about behaviour, so they survive a rewrite — and most apply to Half A too.

Never guess

A wrong number in a money tool is worse than a missing one. Missing, you go look. Wrong, you plan around it — and the first time you catch it lying, the whole thing is dead. Anything the system isn't sure about must say so, in a place you'll see.

Corrections are permanent or they're worthless

If you fix a bad number and any later process reverts it, you'll fix it twice and then never again. A human correction outranks every automated source, forever, no exceptions.

Derived money is computed, never stored

Cache "due on the 26th" and it starts going stale silently. Change a release date and the money should move on the next read, with nothing to re-run. Cheap up front, expensive to retrofit.

Undatable money needs a visible home

There will always be committed money you can't date yet. It can't be dropped and it can't be folded into a real date — both produce a number that's confidently wrong. Own bucket, on screen, with a route to fixing it.

Alert on change, not on state

"You owe $340" every morning gets muted inside a week, and takes the one that mattered with it. Fire on transitions, each once. This applies double to Half A — a monitor that cries wolf is a monitor you'll turn off, and then you miss the drop.

The subtotal trap

The first dollar figure in a receipt is the subtotal. Taking it produces a number wrong by exactly the tax — the worst kind of wrong, because it looks fine. Find the labelled total; tax and shipping ride with the shipment, not the earliest item.

What I'd undo from last time

Where the old methods were wrong, not just incidental. Three are reversals of things I built and would now argue against.

Then: a regex parser per field, tuned against sample emails.

Don't hand-write an extractor

I wrote pattern matching for order numbers, totals and line items. It passes its tests and it is a treadmill — every template change is a bug, every new retailer a project.

Hand the text to a model and ask for structured output. It reads layouts it's never seen, survives redesigns, handles retailers you never planned for, and costs a fraction of a cent a message. Have it return a confidence signal and route the unsure ones to review — same safety valve, no maintenance.

What you give up: determinism. Mitigate by extracting once per message and storing the result, so re-running never rewrites history, and by showing what it extracted so a wrong read is visible rather than silent.

Then: user-authored rules with sender/subject/body conditions.

Rules should be the override, not the on-ramp

You asked for rules and I made them mandatory — nothing worked until you'd authored conditions. Backwards. Those rules only ever answered "is this an order, and from whom," which is a classification question needing no configuration at all.

Invert it: works on day one with empty config, and rules exist for when you disagree — always count this sender, never count that one, always treat these as pre-orders. Same control, without paying for it up front.

Then: poll IMAP on a timer, store app passwords.

Consider making mail arrive instead of fetching it

Polling means credentials at rest, a scan window, a batch size, a memory ceiling — all of it in service of "how do I see the mail."

A forwarding filter in each inbox pointed at one address the system owns deletes that whole category, and gives you a clean way to feed it something by hand. Not automatically right — polling wins if you want a year of history on day one, since forwarding only sees mail from the day you set it up.

Then: five order statuses, retail-flavoured.

There are only two questions

I modelled pre-order / open / shipped / delivered / cancelled. Only two distinctions drive anything: has the money left, and when will it. Model those; let status be a label you compute for display, not a state the logic branches on.

Genuinely yours to decide

Deliberately unanswered — each changes the shape of the build, and last time I picked defaults that hardened into assumptions.

Open

What is a product, exactly?

The shared spine's whole job, and the only question both halves depend on. A retailer SKU? A market identifier? A name you assign? Get this one wrong and the seam never closes. Worth settling before anything else.

Where does each half live?

They don't have to live in the same place, and A4 suggests Half A may not get to choose. Decide them separately.

How do receipts get in?

Fetched, forwarded, or pasted. Pasted-by-hand is a legitimate v1 — it proves the money model with zero infrastructure, and within a week you'd know whether the output justifies automating the input.

Where does the answer surface?

Somewhere you already look, or somewhere you'd have to go. Be honest about which you actually check. A perfect number on a page you open twice a month is worth less than a rough one that reaches you.

How much history on day one?

Starting from today is dramatically simpler than backfilling a year. Ask whether past orders change any decision you'll make, or would just be nice to look at.

Where do release dates come from?

You typing them is the honest v1 and might be permanent — a handful a month. But Half A's pre-drop visibility (A3) is exactly a source for this, which is an argument for building that part of A early.

The trap

Both halves have the same one. The plumbing — mail ingestion on B, retailer polling on A — is the visible part, the fun part, and the part that feels like the project. Neither is the hard part. Both are solved problems with a few known approaches, any of which works.

The hard parts are the money model on B (charge timing, per-item allocation, undatable money, sticky corrections — under a page of logic where every subtle bug lives) and, on A, deciding what's worth alerting about at all. Neither needs any plumbing to build or to test.

Build the money model first, drive it with receipts you paste in by hand, and make it genuinely useful before a line of mail plumbing exists. If it isn't useful with hand-fed data, automating the feed won't rescue it. Last time I built the plumbing early because it was the interesting bit, and the money model came out right mostly by luck. Don't repeat that ordering — and don't repeat it on Half A either.

No schema, no file paths, no stack. If a future version of this contains any of those, it's turned back into a spec — a fine thing to write, but write it second, and against the decisions above.
